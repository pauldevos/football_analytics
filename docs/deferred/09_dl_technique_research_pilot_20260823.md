## 0. Technique/alignment primer

(Merged in from an earlier pass in this pilot whose output was not saved to
disk — reconstructed from conversation record, cross-checked against
PFF and multiple independent coaching-content sites.)

Defensive line "techniques" describe alignment relative to the offensive
line, not nominal position. **Even numbers = head-up on a lineman
(two-gap, flexible); odd numbers = shaded into a gap (one-gap,
pre-committed); an "i" suffix = shaded to the inside eye/shoulder rather
than outside.** Numbering runs outward from the center (0) toward the
sideline (9).

| Technique | Alignment | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Head-up on center | Two-gap NT anchor, minimal pass-rush expectation. Classic 3-4 nose. |
| 1 | Inside shoulder of guard (A-gap) | One-gap run-stuffer, draws center+guard doubles. The 4-3 "nose"/"shade" tackle. |
| 2 | Head-up on guard | Two-gap, flexible A/B-gap; less common as a base call than 1/2i/3. |
| 2i | Inside shoulder of guard, toward center | Interior run-defender in odd-front/3-4 schemes, flanking a true 0-tech nose. |
| 3 | Outside shoulder of guard (B-gap) | Classic interior pass-rush penetrator — quicker, leaner. The 4-3 "three-technique." Prototype: Aaron Donald. |
| 4 | Head-up on tackle | Two-gap tackle-aligned; more of an "under front"/goal-line call than a base 4-3/3-4 assignment. |
| 4i | Inside shoulder of tackle (B-gap) | Odd-front/"over-under" scheme piece; inside leverage disrupts zone blocking. Alan Page roamed 0-to-4i. |
| 5 | Outside shoulder of tackle (C-gap) | Traditionally a two-gap run-first 3-4 end; "5-technique" is essentially synonymous with "3-4 DE" across sources. Prototype: J.J. Watt. |
| 6 | Head-up on tight end | Edge-setter with two-gap-style TE responsibility. Less commonly used as a standalone position label than 5/7. |
| 7 | Inside shoulder of tight end | Edge defender setting the edge while still rushing; common 3-4 OLB or heavier 4-3 DE alignment. |
| 8 | Outside the tackle/TE, wider than standard edge | Niche/rare call, wide-alignment run-force/contain. |
| 9 (incl. "Wide-9") | Outside the TE — widest common alignment | Pure speed pass-rush; minimal run responsibility. Prototype: Von Miller. "Wide-9" is this used as a full-time base call (Jim Washburn-coached fronts). |

**Anchor points every source agrees on**: 0-tech = nose over center,
3-tech = outside guard/B-gap, 5-tech = outside tackle/C-gap. 2i/4i are
real, commonly-used terms, not a niche invention (confirmed across
multiple independent coaching-content sources). 8-technique is real but
thin on documentation relative to 6/7/9.

Sources: [PFF — Pro Defensive Line Techniques: The 2017 Prototypes](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-defensive-line-techniques-the-2017-prototypes) (strongest single source), [PFF — Defensive Prototypes: 9-Technique](https://www.pff.com/news/defensive-prototypes-9-technique), [Bleacher Report — Understanding Defensive Line Techniques](https://bleacherreport.com/articles/285000-understanding-defensive-line-techniques), [Big Blue View glossary](https://www.bigblueview.com/2023/5/24/23734677/defensive-line-techniques-gaps-explained-big-blue-view-glossary-of-terms), [The Phinsider — Football 101: DL gap techniques](https://www.thephinsider.com/2014/3/7/5482102/football-101-defensive-line-gap-techniques), [X&O Labs — Training the 4i Technique Defender](https://www.xandolabs.com/the-lab/defense/front/odd-front-structures/training-the-4i-technique-defender/).

## 1. The corrected principle: situational technique-switching is not ambiguity

**The error to fix**: earlier framing treated a player sliding between two
techniques on passing downs (e.g. "Joe Greene moved between nose and
3-technique depending on the call") as evidence the player lacked a fixed
base identity — logged as "ambiguous / rotating."

**Why that's wrong**: every NFL team, in every era and scheme (4-3 or 3-4),
brings in a lighter pass-rush package on 3rd-and-long — nickel/dime
personnel, interior linemen shifted a gap outside their base alignment to
attack a specific protection. This is normal situational football, not a
refutation of the player's identity. A player's **base technique** (the
alignment he plays on early downs / as his primary run-defense assignment)
is the fact worth encoding; a documented passing-down wrinkle is a footnote
on top of it, not a competing identity.

**Joe Greene, corrected**: Greene's documented, famous base identity is
**1-technique** — shading the inside shoulder of the guard/outside shoulder
of the center, shooting that gap. This is literally what his signature
"Stunt 4-3" innovation *is*: starting in 1974, under DC Bud Carson, Greene
lined up at a sharp angle in the A-gap between center and guard and
exploded upfield through it, occupying (often two or three) blockers and
freeing MLB Jack Lambert to run free. Multiple independent sources describe
this consistently as an angled 1-technique/nose-adjacent alignment, not a
neutral or rotating one. **Corrected classification: 1-technique (base),
with a documented situational passing-down wrinkle — not ambiguous, not
rotating.** Any earlier "refuted" framing for Greene in this pilot's prior
passes should be treated as superseded by this correction.
[Steelers.com — Labriola on Joe Greene's 75th birthday](https://www.steelers.com/news/labriola-on-joe-greene-s-75th-birthday),
[Joe Greene — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Greene)

**Independent cross-validation, found later this pass**: Paul Zimmerman
("Dr. Z," SI's longtime NFL writer) names this exact alignment the
**"Cocked Nose-tackle"** setup in his own writing — Greene attacking the
center-guard gap from an angled/tilted position. This is very likely the
same terminology later used for Michael Dean Perry's "cock nose"
alignment at Cleveland (Section 2 table) — both installed by DC Bud
Carson, who ran the scheme with Greene in Pittsburgh first and carried it
to Cleveland later in his career. Two independent players, two
generations apart, describing the same named scheme concept from the same
coach is strong corroboration. [SI — Dr. Z's All-Time Team: Defense and Special Teams](https://www.si.com/nfl/2016/06/30/dr-z-paul-zimmerman-memoirs-alltime-team-defense-special-teams)

### Corrected epistemic standard: convention + documented role is a real answer, not a guess

The user's direct correction, and it's right: standard 4-3/3-4 gap responsibilities by position and side are extremely well-documented football knowledge (entire coaching books cover exactly this), not obscure trivia. **Unless a player is specifically noted as playing a nonstandard technique for that scheme/side, the standard convention for their documented position (LDT/RDT, LDE/RDE, NT) in that scheme and era *is* what they played, with real confidence — this is the primary method for the large majority of players, not a last-resort fallback.** A missing literal citation of "1-technique" doesn't mean the answer is unknown; it means the answer has to be derived by combining (a) the player's documented position/side, (b) the well-established convention for that slot in that scheme/era (Section 3), and (c) any documented role description, which independently confirms the convention-based answer far more often than it contradicts it. Reserve genuine "Unfound"/unassigned status for cases where the *scheme itself* doesn't map onto standard conventions (the Cowboys' Flex, Section 4) or where side/scheme isn't confidently known at all — not simply because no single source happened to use the numeral.

## 3. Default convention by position/side/era — practical fallback for TCS

This section formalizes the user's proposed convention for the large
majority of players in the corpus who are **not** individually documented
(non-stars, backups, journeymen). Per the user's own reasoning: a fixed,
era-typical division of labor held across most teams by scheme, and
"you won't see [individual documentation] for the guys who were
non-starters — if they had that immense skill, they'd be starting in the
first place." Use these as **defaults**, overridden by any player-specific
documented finding above.

### 4-3 defensive end split (1960s-1980s, standard era)

Two DE spots in a base 4-3 are not interchangeable; they typically carry a
consistent side-based division of labor:

- **LDE ("run side" / strong-side-facing end)** — faces the offense's right
  side (RT, RG) and the quarterback's blind side from the QB's perspective
  in a right-handed-dominant league. Usually the **5-technique** (outside
  shoulder of the tackle) or, when a tight end lines up on that side, kicks
  out to a **7-technique** (inside shoulder of the TE). Profile: stronger
  run defender, sees the tight end frequently, sets the edge.
- **RDE ("blind-side" / pass-rush end)** — rushes the quarterback's blind
  side. Typically the better pure pass rusher of the pair, and rarely faces
  a tight end (TEs align to the offense's right far more often than the
  left in this era's run-heavy sets). Profile: quicker, more speed-rush
  oriented, less consistently a two-gap run defender.

This is a **default**, not a universal law — override it with any
documented individual finding (e.g., a team known to reverse the
assignment, or a player explicitly documented playing the "other" role).

### 4-3 defensive tackle split (already well-established from this pilot)

- **1-technique** — inside shoulder of the guard / outside shoulder of
  center, shading toward the center. Run-anchor, occupies double teams,
  frees the middle linebacker. (Joe Greene, corrected, per Section 1.)
- **3-technique** — outside shoulder of the guard, shaded toward the B-gap.
  Penetrator, primary interior pass-rush threat. (Randle, Sapp, Millard,
  Donald, Kennedy — all directly documented above.)
- These two are **complementary, not independent**: a base 4-3 front needs
  exactly one of each on the interior. If one tackle is documented as
  1-technique, the *other* interior lineman defaults to 3-technique (or an
  adjacent shade, e.g. 2i) unless separately documented — this is the
  structural-inference logic used for Olsen/Grier above, and should be
  applied the same way to any other undocumented 4-3 tackle pairing.

### 3-4 equivalent

- **0-technique nose tackle** — head-up (or slightly shaded) on the center,
  two-gapping by default. (Culp, Fernandez, Baumhower — all directly
  documented above.)
- **5-technique ends (both sides)** — outside shoulder of the tackle,
  two-gap-oriented run defenders by default in a traditional 3-4, unless a
  player is individually documented as a more pass-rush-specialized
  "elephant"/hybrid end (a later-era wrinkle, more common from the 1990s
  3-4 revival onward — do not apply that exception by default to 1960s-80s
  3-4 ends without a specific source).

### How to apply this in TCS

1. Named-star with a documented technique (Section 2 table) → use it
   directly.
2. Non-documented player, known scheme (4-3 or 3-4) and known side/position
   (LDE/RDE, or DT paired with a documented teammate) → apply the default
   convention above.
3. No scheme/position information at all → do not force a technique
   assignment; this convention is a fallback keyed to known
   scheme+side/position, not a way to manufacture technique data from
   nothing.

## 4. Special case: Dallas Cowboys "Flex" defense

Already established in prior passes and reconfirmed this pass: Tom Landry's
Flex defense (Cowboys, 1960s-1980s) is a genuine structural outlier and
should **not** be shoehorned into the standard 4-3 technique-number
convention above. Two of the four Flex linemen were set a half-step off the
line from the other two, with read-and-react gap responsibilities that
don't map cleanly onto a fixed technique numeral the way a standard 4-3 or
3-4 front does. This is very likely part of why no source pins Randy
White (a Flex-era Cowboys DT) to a specific technique number despite his
fame and extensive documentation — the scheme itself resists that encoding,
not a gap in the historical record. **Recommendation: encode Flex-era
Cowboys DL (roughly the Bob Lilly through Randy White/Ed "Too Tall" Jones
years) as their own scheme category in TCS, separate from both the
standard 4-3 and 3-4 conventions, rather than forcing a technique-number
default onto them.**

## 5. Overall recommendation for TCS

**Corrected priority order, per the epistemic-standard fix above** — the
default convention is the *primary* method for the large majority of the
corpus, not a fallback of last resort. A missing literal citation is not
the same thing as an unknown answer.

1. **Tier 1 — documented technique (Section 2 table)**: use directly when
   a source states it explicitly (10 High-confidence rows) or when
   documented role + known convention converge unambiguously (Olsen).
2. **Tier 2 — default convention (Section 3), applied with real
   confidence**: for any player with a known scheme (4-3/3-4) and known
   side/position (LDE/RDE, or a paired-tackle inference against a
   documented teammate), apply the standard convention directly. This is
   expected to cover the large majority of the corpus's volume — treat it
   as a real, historically-grounded answer, not a placeholder pending
   better sourcing.
3. **Tier 3 — Cowboys Flex exception (Section 4)**: encode separately —
   this is the correct kind of unresolved case, where the *scheme itself*
   doesn't map onto standard numbering (Randy White). Don't force Tier 2's
   convention onto a scheme it doesn't apply to.
4. **Tier 4 — genuinely unknown**: reserve for players where even the
   base scheme or side isn't confidently known — not for players who
   simply lack a literal source quote but have known scheme+side+role.
   That's a much smaller bucket than this pilot originally treated it as.
5. Keep Section 1's correction in mind for any player showing apparent
   "rotation" between two techniques: check whether it's a passing-down
   package shift (normal, doesn't disqualify a base-technique assignment)
   before logging it as ambiguous.
